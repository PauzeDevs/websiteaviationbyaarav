Alaska Airlines passengers left on Red Cross cots after Yakima diversion, no hotel assistance provided

A weather‑forced diversion of flight AS322 to Yakima Air Terminal resulted in an overnight stay on Red Cross cots, with the carrier citing the diversion as weather‑related and declining to offer hotel rooms or meal vouchers, according to Simple Flying.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-12 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

On November 7, 2026, Alaska Airlines flight AS322 from Dallas to Seattle was diverted to Yakima due to severe weather and a subsequent fuel‑indicator fault. The Boeing 737‑900ER remained on the ground overnight, and passengers were confined to the terminal where the American Red Cross supplied cots and basic supplies. Alaska Airlines classified the event as a weather‑related diversion and therefore did not provide hotel accommodations or meal vouchers, a decision reported by Simple Flying and not independently verified.

Diversion and technical setback

The aircraft, a Boeing 737‑900ER registered N277AK, left Dallas‑Fort Worth about 50 minutes behind schedule and approached Seattle‑Tacoma International Airport when deteriorating weather forced the crew to abort the landing. The flight was rerouted to Yakima Air Terminal, roughly 100 miles east of Seattle, and touched down shortly after 9 p.m. local time. While ground crews began refueling, engineers identified a malfunctioning fuel‑indicator, which required specialist inspection from Seattle and prevented the plane from departing that night.

Passenger confinement and Red Cross intervention

With 137 passengers and eight crew members on board, the aircraft remained at the gate well into the early hours of November 8. Passengers were not permitted to leave the aircraft until after midnight, after which they were told the flight would not resume until the following day. The airport’s director, Christopher Hopkins, coordinated with the American Red Cross to provide emergency bedding, blankets, toiletries and other necessities. Red Cross volunteers set up cots inside the terminal, where passengers spent the night.

Airline’s accommodation policy

According to the Simple Flying report, an Alaska Airlines representative initially offered hotel rooms, but the offer was withdrawn after midnight. The airline later classified the diversion as weather‑related, a category it treats as outside its contractual obligation to cover lodging or meals. Consequently, Alaska Airlines did not issue hotel vouchers or meal allowances, leaving the Red Cross and airport staff to fill the gap.

Public and media response

The incident attracted coverage from local outlets such as the Yakima Herald‑Republic and was highlighted by NBC Right Now, which noted the prolonged wait on the aircraft and the lack of disembarkation until after 12 a.m. Passengers, including one identified as Michael Pinter, reported the abrupt change in assistance. The airline’s statement, as quoted by Simple Flying, reiterated that the weather‑related nature of the diversion dictated its policy, though no further comment was provided regarding the fuel‑indicator issue.

Key facts

  • Flight AS322 diverted to Yakima on Nov 7, 2026 due to severe weather and a faulty fuel‑indicator
  • Aircraft remained on the ground overnight; passengers stayed in the terminal on Red Cross‑provided cots
  • Alaska Airlines classified the event as weather‑related and declined to offer hotel rooms or meal vouchers
  • Red Cross supplied cots, blankets, toiletries and water for up to 137 stranded passengers

What this means

The episode underscores how airline policies that differentiate between weather‑related and mechanical disruptions can materially affect passenger welfare. While the weather forced the initial diversion, the subsequent technical fault—an equipment issue—prevented the aircraft from departing, yet the airline’s classification remained unchanged. This creates a policy gap where passengers bear the consequences of a mechanical problem without the compensation typically associated with such events. The reliance on external humanitarian aid highlights a shortfall in contingency planning for extended groundings at smaller airports.
