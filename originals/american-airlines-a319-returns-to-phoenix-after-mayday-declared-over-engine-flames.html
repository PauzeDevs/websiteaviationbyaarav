American Airlines A319 Returns to Phoenix After Mayday Declared Over Engine Flames

An American Airlines Airbus A319 bound for Portland declared a Mayday shortly after take‑off from Phoenix when a trailing SkyWest flight reported seeing engine flames; the aircraft landed safely and was withdrawn for inspection, according to Simple Flying.

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that American Airlines flight AA3230, an Airbus A319 with 120 occupants, declared a Mayday after hydraulic alerts and a possible engine fire were reported by a nearby SkyWest crew. The aircraft returned to Phoenix, landed under its own power, and was taken out of service for inspection. Passengers were deplaned and re‑booked on another aircraft for later that evening.

Incident Overview

According to Simple Flying, American Airlines flight AA3230, operating an Airbus A319 from Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport (PHX) to Portland International Airport (PDX), declared a Mayday shortly after departure on November 19, 2026. The crew initially reported hydraulic problems, and a few minutes later, a trailing SkyWest Airlines flight alerted air traffic control that its crew had observed flames emanating from one of the A319’s engines. The Mayday call prompted ATC to vector the aircraft back toward Phoenix for an immediate return.

Timeline and Crew Communications

Simple Flying’s account indicates that after take‑off, the American pilots confirmed hydraulic issues to ATC and requested a return. Within the same period, the SkyWest crew, operating a separate flight, reported visual engine flames to ATC, which was relayed to the American flight’s pilots. Flightdrama, cited by Simple Flying, captured the Mayday transmission: “Mayday, Mayday, Mayday,” followed by a request for vectors back to the departure airport. ATC inquired whether the aircraft needed to burn off fuel; the pilots affirmed the presence of 120 passengers and crew on board.

Response and Aftermath

American Airlines confirmed the incident to Simple Flying, stating that the aircraft landed safely, taxied to the gate under its own power, and was subsequently taken out of service for a thorough inspection by maintenance personnel. Passengers were deplaned in an orderly manner and were scheduled to depart later that evening on a replacement aircraft. The airline’s statement emphasized that safety remained the top priority and apologized for the disruption.

Operational and Safety Implications

While Simple Flying does not provide a definitive cause for the hydraulic alerts or the reported engine flames, the incident underscores the importance of rapid inter‑airline communication and the role of trailing aircraft in identifying potential hazards. The prompt Mayday declaration and coordinated return to the departure airport align with standard emergency procedures for suspected fire or critical system failures. The aircraft’s ability to land and taxi under its own power suggests that any fire was either contained or not present at the time of landing, though the exact status of the engine remains unverified pending inspection results.
