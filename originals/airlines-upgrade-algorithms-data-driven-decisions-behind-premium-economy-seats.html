Airlines’ Upgrade Algorithms: Data‑Driven Decisions Behind Premium Economy Seats

Simple Flying explains that airlines rely on complex ranking systems—factoring ticket class, loyalty status, spending and credit‑card activity—to allocate premium‑economy upgrades, a process that remains largely opaque to passengers.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-12 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

A Simple Flying feature outlines how U.S. carriers such as United and American use proprietary algorithms to decide which passengers receive premium‑economy upgrades. The article identifies five key drivers: ticket type, elite status, upgrade instruments, overall spend (including credit‑card purchases) and ancillary metrics. While the exact formulas are proprietary, the piece notes that loyalty points earned in the prior year can outweigh status alone, and that spending on the ground may tip the balance in a passenger’s favor. The report stresses that these practices are not independently verified and may differ across airlines.

Data‑Driven Upgrade Ranking

According to Simple Flying, airlines have moved away from ad‑hoc, gate‑agent discretion toward systematic, algorithmic ranking. The systems assign scores to each eligible passenger based on a set of weighted criteria. Ticket class—whether a fare is fully flexible or discounted—forms the baseline, while elite status provides a secondary boost. The article emphasizes that the precise weightings are carrier‑specific and not publicly disclosed, making the process appear mysterious to travelers.

Loyalty Status and Upgrade Instruments

The Simple Flying account notes that elite tiers remain a core component of the ranking, but they are now complemented by "upgrade instruments" such as miles, vouchers or paid upgrade offers. Passengers holding the same status are further differentiated by the amount of loyalty points accumulated over the previous twelve months. In American Airlines’ hierarchy, for example, a higher point total can place a traveler ahead of a peer with identical status, effectively turning recent spending into a tiebreaker.

Spending Beyond the Flight

A notable claim from the source is that credit‑card and ancillary spending now influence upgrade eligibility. Airlines track purchases made through co‑branded cards, in‑flight services, and even ground‑based retail. The article cites American Airlines as explicitly using Loyalty Points—earned from both flight and non‑flight spend—as a major factor. Consequently, a single high‑value long‑haul ticket may outrank multiple low‑cost trips when the algorithm evaluates total revenue contribution.

Other Influencing Variables

Simple Flying lists additional, less visible inputs such as seat inventory, flight load factor, and real‑time operational considerations. While the article does not provide exact formulas, it suggests that airlines may prioritize passengers who have purchased upgrade instruments or who have a history of using premium services. The piece also mentions that some carriers publish enough detail for frequent flyers to infer the hierarchy, though the full methodology remains proprietary.

Key facts

  • Airlines employ proprietary algorithms that rank upgrade candidates by ticket type, status, spending and other metrics
  • Loyalty points earned in the past 12 months can outweigh elite status alone in the upgrade queue
  • Credit‑card and ancillary spend are factored into the ranking, potentially outweighing flight frequency
  • Upgrade instruments such as miles, vouchers or paid offers act as additional ranking levers

What this means

The evidence suggests that U.S. airlines are increasingly aligning upgrade allocations with revenue generation rather than pure loyalty. By integrating credit‑card spend and ancillary purchases into the algorithm, carriers reward customers who contribute higher overall dollars, even if their flight mileage is modest. This shift may encourage passengers to consolidate spending with airline‑affiliated financial products, reinforcing a revenue‑centric loyalty model. However, the lack of transparency means travelers cannot reliably predict upgrade outcomes, reinforcing the perception of randomness.

What we could not verify

The report relies solely on a single Simple Flying article; no independent verification of the described algorithms or weighting schemes is available. The article focuses on major U.S. carriers and may not reflect practices of airlines outside the United States or low‑cost carriers. Specific numerical weightings, formulae and carrier‑by‑carrier variations are not disclosed, limiting the precision of the analysis.
