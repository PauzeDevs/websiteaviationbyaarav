United Flight UA2174 Reports Attempted Cockpit Breach and Bomb Threat

A United Airlines Boeing 737 MAX 9 en route from Newark to New Orleans on Nov 9, 2026 experienced a passenger‑initiated attempt to enter the cockpit and claimed a backpack contained a bomb, prompting the crew to squawk 7500 and issue a Level 4 threat alert.

United Airlines flight UA2174, a scheduled Newark‑New Orleans service on a Boeing 737 MAX 9, was disrupted on the evening of Nov 9, 2026 when a male passenger attempted to storm the cockpit and asserted that his backpack held an explosive device. The flight crew responded by transmitting the hijack squawk code 7500 and an ACARS Level 4 threat message to other United aircraft. Incident Overview

According to Simple Flying, United flight UA2174 departed Newark Liberty International Airport at 21:26 UTC and was cruising at approximately 20,000 feet when a male passenger made an attempt to breach the cockpit of the Boeing 737 MAX 9. The passenger reportedly brandished a backpack and claimed it contained a bomb. The crew’s immediate reaction was to activate the hijack transponder code 7500, signalling unlawful interference to air traffic control.

Crew Response and Communication

The pilots also used the ACARS data link to broadcast a Level 4 threat alert—defined by the airline as an attempted or actual cockpit intrusion—to other United aircraft in the vicinity. Simple Flying notes that the ACARS message read, “We had an attempted level four, no other details are known at this time.” The squawk code was entered as the aircraft descended through the 20,000‑foot altitude band, after which the flight proceeded to its scheduled destination without further disruption.

Flight Timeline and Outcome

Flight tracking data cited by Simple Flying shows the aircraft touched down at Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport at 23:07 UTC, roughly two hours after departure. The report states that the flight completed its journey as planned and that no injuries or additional security incidents were reported. Simple Flying indicated that United Airlines had been contacted for comment, but no response was available at the time of publication.

Context and Industry Implications

The incident occurred weeks after a separate United flight was diverted following a passenger threat to hijack the aircraft. Simple Flying’s coverage highlights that such events trigger established security protocols, including the use of the 7500 squawk and Level 4 threat designation, to alert both air traffic control and other airline operators. The report underscores the importance of rapid crew decision‑making and the role of ACARS in disseminating real‑time threat information across an airline’s fleet.
