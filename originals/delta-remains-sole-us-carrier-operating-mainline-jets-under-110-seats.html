Delta Remains Sole US Carrier Operating Mainline Jets Under 110 Seats

Delta’s continued use of the 109‑seat A220‑100 and 110‑seat Boeing 717 reflects a mix of pilot agreements, acquisition cost advantages and niche market needs, according to Simple Flying.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-15 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Delta Air Lines is the only major U.S. airline that still flies mainline aircraft with fewer than 110 seats – the Airbus A220‑100 and the Boeing 717. Simple Flying attributes this unique fleet composition to a combination of labor contracts, favorable purchase pricing and the airline’s strategy to serve lower‑demand domestic routes. The article notes that the gradual retirement of the 717 raises questions about how Delta will sustain service to these markets as its fleet evolves.

Unique Position in the U.S. Market

Simple Flying reports that Delta’s mainline fleet includes two aircraft types that sit at the low end of the narrow‑body spectrum: the 109‑seat Airbus A220‑100 and the 110‑seat Boeing 717. No other major U.S. carrier operates mainline jets of comparable size, relying instead on larger narrow‑bodies for their core networks while outsourcing smaller regional jets to partners under the Delta Connection brand.

Labor Agreements and Acquisition Costs

According to the source, pilot agreements that allow Delta to staff these smaller jets with its own crew, rather than outsourcing to regional partners, are a key factor. The article also highlights that Delta was able to purchase the aircraft at “affordable rates,” which helps offset the higher per‑seat operating costs typically associated with low‑capacity planes.

Operational Role and Market Fit

Delta uses the A220‑100 and the remaining 717s to serve routes where passenger demand does not justify a larger aircraft. Simple Flying points out that this niche capability enables the airline to maintain service to smaller domestic markets without resorting to regional affiliates, preserving brand consistency and potentially offering a smoother customer experience.

Future Outlook and Uncertainties

The Simple Flying piece notes that the Boeing 717 is being phased out, creating uncertainty about how Delta will continue to serve the smallest markets. The article does not provide details on replacement plans, leaving open whether Delta will acquire additional A220‑100s, shift more traffic to regional partners, or explore other aircraft types.

Key facts

  • Delta is the only major U.S. airline operating mainline jets with fewer than 110 seats
  • The fleet includes a 109‑seat Airbus A220‑100 and a 110‑seat Boeing 717
  • Pilot agreements and low acquisition costs are cited as primary enablers
  • The 717 is being retired, raising questions about future small‑market service

What this means

The evidence suggests that Delta’s small‑jet strategy is driven less by passenger demand alone and more by the interplay of labor contracts and cost‑effective aircraft procurement. By keeping these aircraft in the mainline fleet, Delta retains direct operational control, which may be advantageous for brand perception and service consistency on thin routes. However, the pending retirement of the 717 introduces a strategic inflection point; without a clear replacement, Delta could either expand its A220‑100 fleet, increase reliance on regional partners, or adjust its network to concentrate on higher‑density corridors.
