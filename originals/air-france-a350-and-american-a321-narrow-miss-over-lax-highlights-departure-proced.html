Air France A350 and American A321 Narrow Miss Over LAX Highlights Departure Procedure Risks

A near‑midair collision involving an Air France A350‑900 bound for Papeete and an American Airlines A321 headed to Dallas unfolded minutes after takeoff from Los Angeles International Airport, prompting immediate ATC intervention.

At a glance

Two wide‑body jets departing Los Angeles International Airport on a Saturday morning came within 400 feet of vertical separation after the Air France Airbus A350‑900 deviated from its assigned climb path. Controllers and pilots acted quickly to avoid a collision. The incident underscores the importance of strict adherence to departure procedures such as the FAA‑mandated SUMMR2 route.

Incident Overview

According to Simple Flying, the event occurred in the early hours of Saturday, 8 November 2026, when Air France flight 28, an Airbus A350‑900 destined for Papeete, and American Airlines flight 1660, an Airbus A321 bound for Dallas/Fort Worth, departed Los Angeles International Airport (LAX) on parallel runways. The A350 was cleared for the SUMMR2 departure procedure, which requires aircraft to climb straight ahead before executing any turn. Shortly after takeoff, the A350 made an unauthorized left turn that intersected the climb corridor of the American A321. The two aircraft approached within 400 feet (approximately 121 metres) of vertical separation. Air traffic controllers detected the conflict and issued corrective instructions, averting a potential collision. Both aircraft together could have been carrying more than 500 passengers.

Operational Context and Procedure

The SUMMR2 route, as defined by the Federal Aviation Administration, is a standardized departure path for runways 24L/R and 25L/R at LAX. Its purpose is to separate departing traffic vertically and laterally until aircraft reach a safe altitude for en‑route navigation. Simple Flying notes that the Air France A350 was expected to maintain a straight‑ahead climb before turning, a step it failed to follow. The American A321 was following its own assigned climb profile, which placed it on a trajectory that intersected the A350’s unexpected turn. The rapid response by ATC and the flight crews prevented the aircraft from breaching minimum separation standards.

Safety Response and Immediate Aftermath

Following the near miss, controllers instructed the A350 to resume its proper climb and the A321 to adjust its flight path, restoring the required separation. No injuries were reported, and both flights continued to their respective destinations. Simple Flying’s report does not indicate whether any formal investigation has been launched, but standard practice would involve the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) and the FAA reviewing ATC recordings, flight data, and cockpit voice recordings to determine the root cause of the procedural deviation.

Broader Implications for Departure Management

The incident illustrates how a single deviation from a prescribed departure route can quickly cascade into a high‑risk situation, especially at busy hubs like LAX where multiple runways operate simultaneously. It reinforces the need for rigorous pilot training on departure procedures, real‑time monitoring by ATC, and robust safety nets such as automated conflict detection systems. While the immediate response was effective, the event may prompt airlines and regulators to reassess compliance monitoring and to consider additional safeguards for high‑traffic departure corridors.
