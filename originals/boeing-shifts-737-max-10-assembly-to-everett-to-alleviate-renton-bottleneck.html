Boeing Shifts 737 MAX 10 Assembly to Everett to Alleviate Renton Bottleneck

The first 737 MAX 10 rolled off Boeing’s new North Line in Everett on Oct. 26, 2026, ending a decades‑long tradition of building every 737 variant in Renton and addressing spatial constraints that threatened production flexibility.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-06 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that Boeing moved the assembly of its longest 737 MAX variant to the Everett plant’s newly opened North Line after discovering that the existing Renton layout could not accommodate the 143‑ft‑8‑in airframe without staggering it between shorter models. The shift aims to remove scheduling friction, support the 1,555 firm orders for the MAX 10, and restore the ability to run a continuous nose‑to‑tail line.

Background and First Roll‑Out

According to Simple Flying, Boeing rolled the first 737 MAX 10 out of the Everett facility’s North Line on Oct. 26, 2026. The aircraft is destined for WestJet, marking the first time since 1970 that a 737 variant has been assembled outside the historic Renton plant. The move reflects a strategic response to the MAX 10’s dimensions – a fuselage length of 143 feet 8 inches (43.8 m), which exceeds the spatial envelope of Renton’s compact assembly bays.

Operational Constraints at Renton

The Simple Flying article explains that Renton’s three active assembly lines occupy roughly 1.1 million sq ft (102,000 m²) bounded by Lake Washington and nearby rail lines. Standard 737 MAX 8s, at 129 ft 6 in (39.5 m), fit comfortably within this footprint. However, the MAX 10 is 14 ft 2 in (4.3 m) longer, causing its nose and tail sections to intrude on overhead crane pathways and the working envelopes of adjacent stations. To avoid structural collisions, Renton line managers must manually stagger the build sequence, inserting shorter MAX 7 or MAX 8 airframes before and after each MAX 10. This practice limits production flexibility and creates scheduling friction for the plant’s 12,000‑person workforce.

Strategic Shift to Everett’s North Line

Simple Flying notes that the new North Line in Everett provides a dedicated, wide‑body‑scale assembly area tailored to the MAX 10’s length. By relocating the longest narrow‑body to Everett, Boeing can run a continuous nose‑to‑tail line without the need for staggered sequencing. The article cites Boeing’s own data of 1,555 firm orders for the MAX 10, representing about 31 % of the total 737 MAX backlog, underscoring the urgency of eliminating the Renton bottleneck. The shift also restores the ability to prioritize orders based on demand rather than spatial constraints.

What this means

The relocation of MAX 10 assembly to Everett addresses a clear geometric limitation at Renton that forced the company to intermix longer and shorter airframes. By providing a line sized for the stretched fuselage, Boeing can eliminate the manual staggering process that currently hampers scheduling efficiency. If the North Line can sustain a steady flow, the plant should be able to meet the sizable order book for the MAX 10 without the production bottlenecks that have historically constrained output at Renton. This operational change may also free Renton capacity for higher‑volume, shorter variants, potentially improving overall 737 MAX throughput.
