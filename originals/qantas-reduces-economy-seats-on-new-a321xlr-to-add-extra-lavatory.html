Qantas Reduces Economy Seats on New A321XLR to Add Extra Lavatory

The Australian carrier reconfigured its first long‑range narrow‑body jet, swapping three economy seats for a fourth bathroom after passenger backlash, a move reported solely by Simple Flying.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-12 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Qantas has altered the cabin layout of its inaugural Airbus A321XLR, removing three economy seats to install an additional restroom. The change, affecting the first aircraft of a planned 48‑seat sub‑fleet, responds to criticism over limited toilet facilities compared with low‑cost rivals. The airline also plans to retrofit the earlier three A321XLRs during heavy maintenance. The report is based exclusively on Simple Flying and has not been independently verified.

Background and Fleet Plans

Qantas placed an order for Airbus A321XLRs in 2022, intending to replace its aging Boeing 737‑800s with a longer‑range narrow‑body option. Commercial service with the type began in September 2025, initially offering a configuration of 180 economy seats and 20 business seats. The first aircraft, registered VH‑OGD, is part of a 48‑seat sub‑fleet that Qantas intends to expand, with the airline promoting the model’s wider cabin and fuel efficiency.

Cabin Reconfiguration Details

Following the launch, Qantas received immediate and sharply critical feedback regarding the limited number of toilets in the economy cabin. In response, the airline removed three economy seats and added a fourth lavatory, raising the total economy capacity to 197 seats. The revised layout replaces a row of seats that would have otherwise occupied the space now dedicated to the extra bathroom. Simple Flying notes that the airline intends to retrofit the first three A321XLRs already in service with the additional toilet during future heavy‑maintenance checks.

Industry and Passenger Reaction

Commentators and media outlets highlighted the disparity between Qantas’s original toilet‑to‑passenger ratio and that of low‑cost carriers such as Ryanair, Scoot and Jetstar. A standard Ryanair Boeing 737 carries 197 passengers with three toilets, averaging 66 passengers per facility; Scoot’s Airbus A320 has 180 passengers with three toilets (60 per facility); Jetstar’s A320 seats 186 with three toilets (62 per facility). By contrast, Qantas’s initial configuration offered only two toilets for 180 seats, equating to 90 passengers per facility, a figure that drew criticism and was described as a “legacy stink” linked to the airline’s former CEO. The addition of a fourth bathroom aligns Qantas more closely with competitor ratios.

Future Outlook

The decision underscores a tension between maximizing revenue‑generating seats and ensuring passenger comfort on long‑range narrow‑body routes. Qantas’s commitment to retrofit existing aircraft suggests a broader strategy to standardise the cabin across its A321XLR fleet. Observers will watch whether the added lavatory improves passenger satisfaction and how the seat reduction impacts the airline’s economics, especially given the high utilization rates expected for the type.

Key facts

  • Qantas removed three economy seats from its first A321XLR to install a fourth lavatory
  • The revised cabin now seats 197 economy passengers instead of the original 180
  • Initial configuration offered only two toilets for 180 seats, a ratio of 90 passengers per facility
  • Retrofit of the first three A321XLRs with the extra bathroom is planned during heavy maintenance

What this means

The cabin alteration reflects Qantas’s responsiveness to passenger comfort concerns, prioritising amenity over marginal seat revenue. By aligning its toilet‑to‑passenger ratio with that of low‑cost rivals, the airline may mitigate negative publicity and improve the long‑haul narrow‑body experience, though the financial impact of fewer seats remains to be quantified.
