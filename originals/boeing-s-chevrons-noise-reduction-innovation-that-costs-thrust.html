Boeing’s Chevrons: Noise‑Reduction Innovation That Costs Thrust

An examination of the serrated engine‑exhaust nozzles on the 737 MAX, 747‑8 and 787 Dreamliner reveals a trade‑off between community‑friendly sound levels and measurable performance penalties, a dilemma not shared by rival manufacturers.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-15 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that Boeing’s chevron‑lined engine nacelles, introduced to meet stricter airport noise rules, lower acoustic emissions but also increase drag and reduce thrust across all flight phases. The design, developed with General Electric and NASA, appears unique to Boeing’s current wide‑body and narrow‑body families, and the company has reportedly moved away from the feature after recognizing its efficiency costs.

Background and Development

According to Simple Flying, Boeing’s chevron technology emerged from a collaboration with General Electric and NASA as airlines confronted tighter noise regulations and heightened community pressure around major hubs. The serrated trailing‑edge pattern was first applied commercially on the 787 Dreamliner before being extended to the 747‑8 and the 737 MAX family. The primary objective was to meet airport noise limits while preserving passenger comfort during take‑off and landing.

Design Intent and Operational Benefits

The chevron consists of a saw‑tooth pattern cut into the rear edge of the engine nozzle, creating a mixing effect that disrupts the exhaust plume. Simple Flying notes that this aerodynamic mixing reduces the acoustic signature of the engines, especially during low‑altitude operations. All 787 variants—whether powered by Rolls‑Royce Trent 1000 or General Electric GEnx engines—feature chevrons, and the design is also present on every 737 MAX variant and the 747‑8.

Performance Penalties and Boeing’s Response

While the noise‑reduction goal was achieved, Simple Flying highlights a consequential trade‑off: the same mixing effect that dampens sound also introduces additional drag and a modest thrust loss throughout the flight envelope, including cruise where noise mitigation offers limited benefit. Over time, Boeing reportedly reassessed the cost‑benefit balance and has begun abandoning chevrons in newer designs, seeking alternatives that preserve performance without sacrificing acoustic compliance.

Industry Context

The article emphasizes that no other major modern jet manufacturer has adopted a comparable chevron system, suggesting that Boeing’s approach was singular in the market. This uniqueness underscores both the innovative ambition and the operational compromise inherent in the design, positioning the chevron as a distinctive, albeit contentious, element of Boeing’s recent aircraft portfolio.

Key facts

  • Chevrons were introduced to cut airport‑near noise on the 787, 747‑8 and 737 MAX.
  • The serrated nozzle design creates aerodynamic mixing that reduces sound but adds drag and reduces thrust.
  • All 787 variants, regardless of engine supplier, incorporate chevrons; the feature is also on every 737 MAX and 747‑8.
  • Boeing has reportedly begun phasing out chevrons after recognizing the performance penalty.
  • No other major jet manufacturer currently uses a comparable chevron system.

What this means

The Simple Flying account suggests that Boeing’s chevron solution solved a regulatory noise problem at the expense of aerodynamic efficiency. The trade‑off appears significant enough that Boeing is moving away from the feature, indicating that the performance loss outweighs the community‑noise benefit in the airline’s cost calculations. The lack of adoption by competitors may reflect either differing noise‑mitigation strategies or a judgment that the efficiency hit is not justified.
