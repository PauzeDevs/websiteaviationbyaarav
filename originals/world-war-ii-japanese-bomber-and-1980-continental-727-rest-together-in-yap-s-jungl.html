World War II Japanese bomber and 1980 Continental 727 rest together in Yap’s jungle

A Simple Flying feature documents how a Mitsubishi G4M ‘Betty’ and a burned‑out Boeing 727 lie side‑by‑side amid reclaimed forest on the Micronesian island of Yap, a relic of both wartime and civil‑aviation history.

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that on the island of Yap, the wreckage of a Japanese Mitsubishi G4M ‘Betty’ bomber from World II and the burned remains of a Continental Airlines Boeing 727 that crashed in 1980 are now visible in the same jungle clearing. The two aircraft, separated by decades, have become a unique point of interest for visitors and a reminder of the island’s layered aviation past.

Historical backdrop of Yap’s airfield

According to Simple Flying, Yap’s original airfield was constructed by the Japanese during the Second World War and later bombed by United States forces. After the war, the jungle reclaimed the site, leaving behind scattered aircraft wrecks, gun emplacements and bunkers. The island, now part of the Federated States of Micronesia, was administered by Japan under the League of Nations South Seas Mandate after Japan seized the territory from Germany in the First World War. While nearby Chuuk (Truk) served as a major Japanese naval base, Yap was only lightly defended until later in the Pacific campaign. A United States Navy report from May 1944 cited orders from the commander of the 22nd Air Flotilla at Chuuk to station the 2nd Attack Force and other units on Yap. Subsequent U.S. sorties recorded a mix of Japanese fighters, dive bombers, medium bombers and night fighters operating from the island.

The 1980 Continental 727 accident

Simple Flying states that on an unspecified date in 1980, a Continental Airlines (operating as Air Micronesia) Boeing 727, Flight 614, crashed while attempting to land at the modern Yap International Airport. The aircraft burned out on impact, but, according to the article, all passengers survived. The wreckage settled adjacent to a Mitsubishi G4M ‘Betty’ bomber that had been abandoned on the wartime airfield years earlier. The report does not provide further details on the cause of the crash, the number of occupants, or the exact location of the impact relative to the runway.

The Japanese ‘Betty’ bomber remains

The same Simple Flying piece notes that the Mitsubishi G4M ‘Betty’ bomber, a twin‑engine medium bomber used by the Imperial Japanese Navy, remains in the jungle near the old airfield. The author, Sumit Singh, was guided to the site by a local volunteer pilot named Amos Collins, who helped locate both wrecks after the journalist’s own search proved unsuccessful. The article describes the bomber as still recognizable despite decades of exposure and vegetation growth, offering a tangible link to the island’s wartime role.

Current visibility and tourism

Today, the functional Yap International Airport operates alongside the overgrown remnants of the wartime airfield. Simple Flying reports that visitors can walk to the clearing where the two aircraft lie side‑by‑side, a scene the author describes as “surreal.” The juxtaposition of a 1940s Japanese bomber and a 1980s commercial jet provides a unique attraction for aviation enthusiasts and travelers exploring Micronesia’s remote islands. No formal tourism infrastructure or interpretive signage is mentioned in the source.

What this means

The co‑location of a wartime Japanese bomber and a relatively recent commercial jet illustrates how remote Pacific islands can serve as long‑term repositories for disparate aviation histories. The Simple Flying account suggests that the lack of development around the old airfield allowed nature to preserve both aircraft, turning the site into an informal open‑air museum.
