Hollywood Burbank Airport Tower Goes ‘ATC Zero’ Twice, Pilots Self‑Coordinate Takeoffs and Landings

Pilots from Southwest and Alaska Airlines used the common traffic advisory frequency after the Burbank tower lost controllers for two 30‑minute periods on Wednesday evening, a situation attributed by sources to staffing constraints but not confirmed by the FAA or airport officials.

At a glance

On the evening of 19 November 2026, the control tower at Hollywood Burbank Airport (BUR) experienced two separate intervals without on‑site air traffic controllers. During each interval, pilots switched to the CTAF and announced their positions, a practice normally reserved for uncontrolled fields. The cause of the outages remains unverified, though unnamed sources point to staffing shortages and mandatory rest breaks for a single controller on duty. This marks the second such incident within a little over a year, following a six‑hour shutdown in October 2025 linked to a federal government shutdown.

Incident Overview

Simple Flying reported that the Burbank tower entered an ‘ATC Zero’ status on Wednesday evening, meaning no controllers were present to manage aircraft movements. Recordings from the tower’s audio system captured a controller informing pilots that radar services were ending and that they should transition to the Common Traffic Advisory Frequency (CTAF). The tower remained without staff for roughly 30 minutes on two separate occasions, creating a brief window in which pilots had to manage their own sequencing for takeoff and landing.

Pilot Response on CTAF

During the outage, pilots from Southwest Airlines and Alaska Airlines were heard announcing their positions on the shared CTAF, a protocol typically used at airports without an operating control tower. A Southwest crew reported being on short final for Runway 8, while an Alaska Airlines crew communicated taxiing from the terminal area to a holding pad. A regional controller, still on duty for the broader airspace, continued to vector aircraft toward Burbank before directing them to the CTAF for the final approach phase. The self‑coordination appeared orderly, with pilots acknowledging each other's transmissions and maintaining separation.

Possible Causes and Historical Context

The article notes that neither the Federal Aviation Administration nor Burbank Airport has officially explained the tower closures. An unnamed source cited by CBS LA suggested staffing shortages, specifically that a lone controller may have been required to take mandatory rest breaks, prompting the temporary shutdowns. Simple Flying also referenced a prior incident in October 2025, when the tower was closed for nearly six hours due to staff absences tied to a federal government shutdown. The current event is therefore the second documented ATC Zero occurrence at BUR within a little over a year.

Operational Implications

While the immediate safety impact appeared limited—pilots successfully used CTAF procedures—the repeated loss of tower services raises questions about staffing resilience at midsize airports. The reliance on a single controller for an entire tower makes the facility vulnerable to mandatory rest periods and unexpected absences. Industry observers note that such gaps could increase workload for pilots and regional controllers, potentially elevating the risk of communication errors, especially during peak traffic periods.

What this means

The incident underscores a structural vulnerability at Hollywood Burbank Airport: a single‑controller staffing model that cannot sustain continuous coverage when rest requirements intervene. Although pilots managed the situation using standard uncontrolled‑airport procedures, the reliance on ad‑hoc coordination could strain pilot workload and increase the potential for miscommunication. If similar staffing constraints recur, the airport may need to consider staffing redundancies, cross‑training, or procedural adjustments to ensure uninterrupted tower services, thereby preserving safety margins during peak operations.
