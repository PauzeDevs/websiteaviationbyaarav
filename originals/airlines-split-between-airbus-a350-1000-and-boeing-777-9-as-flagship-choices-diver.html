Airlines Split Between Airbus A350‑1000 and Boeing 777‑9 as Flagship Choices Diverge

Simple Flying reports that differing fleet ages, delivery schedules and strategic priorities are driving carriers such as Air Canada, Emirates, Qatar Airways and United to adopt opposite wide‑body flagships, with the A350‑1000 gaining ground while the 777‑9 remains delayed.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-09 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

A review of Simple Flying’s recent coverage shows a clear bifurcation in flagship aircraft strategy among major carriers. While carriers like Air Canada, Japan Airlines and Qatar Airways are building long‑haul networks around the Airbus A350‑1000, others such as Emirates, United and Lufthansa continue to rely on the Boeing 777 family, despite the 777‑9’s certification delays. The split reflects fleet age, order backlogs, route needs and even engine considerations, rather than brand loyalty alone.

Flagship Paths: A350‑1000 vs 777‑9

According to Simple Flying, airlines are not simply choosing between Airbus and Boeing out of habit. Air Canada is set to introduce eight A350‑1000s in the latter half of 2030, marking its first Airbus wide‑body acquisition in almost thirty years. In contrast, Emirates, the world’s largest 777 operator, has expanded its 777‑9 order by 65 aircraft, bringing its total to 270, with the first 777‑9 expected to arrive in the second quarter of 2027. Qatar Airways, the launch customer for the A350‑1000, entered service with the type in February 2018, while United Airlines continues to operate close to 100 777s and has no A350s in its fleet.

Why the Divergence?

Simple Flying attributes the split to a mix of operational timing, fleet renewal cycles and specific performance needs. Airlines with aging 777‑300ERs, such as Delta Air Lines and Air Canada, cannot afford to wait for Boeing’s delayed 777‑9 deliveries and therefore turn to the A350‑1000, which already has a robust delivery pipeline. Conversely, carriers with relatively young 777 fleets, like Emirates, can sustain their existing aircraft while awaiting the newer model. Additional factors include route capacity requirements, engine commonality and the strategic desire to lock in future capacity ahead of certification milestones.

Current Order and Delivery Landscape

By June 2026, the A350‑1000 had accumulated 367 orders and 114 deliveries, according to Simple Flying’s data. The 777‑9, despite its first flight in January 2020, remains uncertified and is projected to be delivered roughly seven years later than originally scheduled. Lufthansa is slated to be the first airline to operate the 777‑9 in 2027, while American Airlines is retrofitting its existing 777‑300ERs rather than ordering new models. Emirates’ fleet composition underscores its dual strategy: it already operates about 140 777s—including 119 777‑300ERs, 10 777‑200LRs and 11 freighters—while also adding A350s to its roster.

Implications for the Wide‑Body Market

The divergent flagship strategies suggest a market where both aircraft families can coexist, each serving distinct airline needs. Simple Flying notes that the A350‑1000’s growing order book and steady delivery rate make it attractive for carriers seeking immediate capacity expansion. Meanwhile, the 777‑9’s delayed certification may limit its appeal to airlines that can tolerate longer wait times or that already possess a substantial 777 base. The ongoing competition could pressure both manufacturers to accelerate certification processes and offer more flexible delivery options.

What this means

The evidence points to a strategic split where airlines with older 777 fleets prioritize the immediately available A350‑1000 to avoid capacity gaps, while operators with large, relatively new 777 fleets, such as Emirates, continue to invest in the 777‑9 despite its certification lag. This pattern reflects a pragmatic approach focused on fleet renewal timing rather than brand allegiance, and it may sustain demand for both aircraft types well into the next decade.
