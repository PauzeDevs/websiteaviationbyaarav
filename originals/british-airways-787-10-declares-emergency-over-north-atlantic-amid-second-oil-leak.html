British Airways 787-10 Declares Emergency Over North Atlantic Amid Second Oil Leak in Ten Days

A British Airways Dreamliner (G‑ZBLC) reported an engine oil leak on a transatlantic flight to Boston, squawking 7700 and landing safely after a controlled descent, marking the aircraft's second oil‑related incident within ten days, according to Simple Flying.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-25 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that British Airways flight BA239, operated by a Boeing 787‑10 Dreamliner (registration G‑ZBLC), declared a general emergency over the North Atlantic on 24 November 2026 after detecting an engine oil leak. The crew reduced thrust, descended to 28,000 ft, and completed the eight‑hour journey to Boston Logan International Airport, landing safely at 10:55 PM EST—about an hour later than scheduled. The incident follows a similar oil‑related event ten days earlier that forced a diversion to Athens.

Flight Details and Emergency Declaration

According to Simple Flying, BA239 departed London Heathrow shortly after 19:30 local time on 24 November 2026 bound for Boston. Approximately four hours into the roughly eight‑hour crossing, the crew squawked 7700, signalling a general emergency. At the time of the declaration the aircraft was cruising near 33,000 ft and travelling at 378 knots. Pilots reported to Gander ATC that the engine was losing oil when operating at high thrust. In response, they reduced power, which appeared to stabilize the leak, and began a controlled descent to 28,000 ft for the remainder of the flight.

Maintenance Recommendations and Diversion Decision

Simple Flying notes that ACARS messages indicated British Airways maintenance had advised a diversion to Keflavik International Airport (KEF) in Iceland. The flight crew, however, elected to continue to the original destination, Boston, after lowering thrust and descending. The aircraft arrived at Logan International Airport at 22:55 EST, roughly one hour later than the scheduled 21:50 arrival, having flown at a reduced speed below 400 knots compared with the typical cruise speed of close to 500 knots.

Recent History of the Aircraft

The same Boeing 787‑10 (G‑ZBLC) experienced a separate oil‑related incident ten days earlier. Simple Flying reports that the aircraft suffered an engine shutdown over the Mediterranean and was forced to divert to Athens, Greece. The Boston‑bound flight represented the airframe's first scheduled operation after returning to service following that diversion.

Operational Impact and Safety Considerations

Both incidents were resolved without injury, and the aircraft landed safely each time. Simple Flying records indicate the Boston flight’s arrival delay was limited to about an hour. The recurrence of oil‑leak symptoms on the same airframe within a short interval raises questions about the underlying cause, prompting the airline to monitor the aircraft closely. No official statement from British Airways or regulatory bodies is included in the source material.

What this means

The repeated oil‑leak occurrence on the same 787‑10 within a ten‑day window suggests a potential systemic issue with that aircraft’s engine or oil system, rather than an isolated anomaly. British Airways’ decision to forgo the recommended diversion in favor of continuing to Boston reflects confidence in the crew’s ability to manage the reduced‑thrust condition, yet it also underscores the operational trade‑off between schedule adherence and precautionary grounding. The incident highlights the importance of thorough post‑maintenance checks and real‑time monitoring of engine parameters on long‑haul flights.
