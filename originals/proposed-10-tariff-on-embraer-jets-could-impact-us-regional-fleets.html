Proposed 10% Tariff on Embraer Jets Could Impact US Regional Fleets

A U.S. trade proposal to levy a 10% duty on Brazilian‑made aircraft may add hundreds of millions to the price of Embraer jets already ordered by American carriers, according to Simple Flying.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-26 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that the U.S. Trade Representative has announced a draft 10% tariff on finished aircraft imported from Brazil. The measure, which follows a July 25% tariff on other Brazilian goods, would affect more than 200 firm orders and additional purchase options for Embraer jets held by U.S. airlines. At list price, those orders exceed $13 billion, though actual contract values are lower after discounts. The proposal has not been finalized, and its timing could influence future deliveries. Embraer and Brazilian officials have voiced opposition, warning of broader repercussions for the global aerospace supply chain.

Tariff proposal and regulatory context

The Office of the U.S. Trade Representative disclosed a draft 10% duty on finished aircraft from Brazil on 25 November 2026. This move amends the Section 301 tariff regime that already imposed a 25% levy on a range of Brazilian products earlier in the year, while aircraft had previously been exempt. The trade official cited insufficient progress by Brazil on issues raised in the investigation as justification for the added leverage.

Scale of U.S. Embraer commitments

Data cited by Simple Flying, derived from ch‑aviation, indicate that U.S. carriers collectively hold over 200 firm orders for Embraer models, plus roughly 100 additional purchase rights. The backlog as of 30 September showed 163 E175s on firm order for three U.S. customers: American Airlines (66), SkyWest (63) and Republic Airways (26). Avelo Airlines holds options for 50 E195‑E2 jets. When purchase rights are added, the total exposure rises to more than 340 aircraft.

Potential cost implications

Applying the 10% tariff to the list‑price value of the firm orders—estimated at more than $13 billion—could translate into an additional $1.3 billion in duties for U.S. airlines, assuming the tariff is applied at list price. Because actual contract prices are typically discounted, the real financial impact would be lower, but the exact figure remains uncertain. The timing of the tariff’s implementation could also affect aircraft scheduled for delivery in 2027 and beyond, potentially prompting airlines to renegotiate contracts or defer orders.

Stakeholder reactions

Embraer’s public statement, as reported by Simple Flying, indicated that the company is reviewing the proposal and continues to advocate for the zero‑tariff regime that has existed since the 1980s. The planemaker’s share price fell about 5% after the announcement. Brazil’s Foreign Ministry said it would assess the measure’s consequences, while the USTR’s Jamieson Greer emphasized the need for leverage to address what he described as Brazil’s “unreasonable acts, policies, and practices.” No independent confirmation of these reactions is available beyond the Simple Flying article.

Key facts

  • U.S. airlines hold over 200 firm orders for Embraer jets worth more than $13 billion at list price
  • Embraer’s backlog includes 163 E175s for three U.S. customers and options for 50 E195‑E2 jets
  • The proposed tariff could add roughly $1.3 billion in duties if applied at list price
  • Embraer’s shares dropped about 5% after the tariff announcement

What this means

If enacted, the tariff would raise the acquisition cost of Embraer regional jets for U.S. carriers, potentially prompting airlines to seek price concessions, shift to alternative manufacturers, or delay fleet expansion. The exposure is concentrated among regional operators that rely heavily on the E175 platform, suggesting that any cost increase could affect capacity planning for short‑haul routes. However, the actual financial burden will depend on the final tariff language, the timing of deliveries, and the discount levels embedded in existing contracts. Embraer’s opposition underscores the broader industry concern that eroding the long‑standing zero‑tariff environment could set a precedent for future trade actions affecting aerospace supply chains.
