Boeing’s 747‑8 and 787 Dreamliner share a common engine family but use distinct variants

Both Boeing wide‑bodies are powered by GE’s GEnx turbofan line, yet the 747‑8 carries the GEnx‑2B while the 787 relies on the GEnx‑1B, reflecting divergent design needs.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-21 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that the Boeing 747‑8 and 787 Dreamliner are equipped with engines from the same GEnx family. While the core of the turbofan is shared, each aircraft uses a tailored version – the GEnx‑2B for the 747‑8 and the GEnx‑1B for the 787 – resulting in differences in size, power, and system integration. The article highlights how this commonality can simplify maintenance for operators that fly both types, but also notes the technical adjustments required for each airframe’s distinct architecture.

Engine family commonality

According to Simple Flying, both the 747‑8 and the 787 draw power from GE Aviation’s GEnx series. The two engines share the same high‑pressure core and roughly 80 % of their line‑replaceable units, meaning the fundamental hardware is largely identical across the family. This shared architecture was chosen to bring modern fuel‑efficiency and noise‑reduction technologies to both aircraft.

Variant differences: GEnx‑1B versus GEnx‑2B

The 787 is fitted with the GEnx‑1B, described as a larger and more electric‑focused engine. It supports the Dreamliner’s bleed‑less design, where most aircraft systems are powered electrically. In contrast, the 747‑8 uses the GEnx‑2B, a slightly smaller version that retains traditional bleed‑air ports to service the older environmental‑control and engine‑start systems. The 2B also has fewer stages in its booster and low‑pressure turbine, reflecting the different thrust and integration requirements of the jumbo jet.

Operational impact for airlines

Simple Flying notes that airlines operating both types benefit from a common engine family. Shared spare parts, similar maintenance procedures, and overlapping technician training can reduce logistical complexity and cost. However, the distinct variants mean that airlines must still stock specific components for each model, particularly those related to the bleed‑air system on the 747‑8.

Performance, reliability and market reception

Both GEnx variants are claimed to stay on wing about 20 % longer than previous generation engines, with some operators reporting upwards of 30,000 flight hours before a major overhaul. The article cites a GE Aviation product‑line manager who said the GEnx program became the fastest‑selling and fastest‑ramped engine line in the company’s history, delivering roughly 15 % better fuel efficiency compared with the older CF6 engines they replaced.

Key facts

  • Both Boeing 747‑8 and 787 use engines from GE’s GEnx family
  • The 747‑8 is powered by the GEnx‑2B, while the 787 uses the GEnx‑1B
  • GEnx‑1B supports a bleed‑less, electrically driven aircraft architecture
  • GEnx‑2B retains bleed‑air ports for the 747‑8’s traditional systems
  • The core engine and about 80 % of replaceable parts are common to both variants

What this means

The shared engine lineage illustrates Boeing’s strategy of leveraging a common powerplant to modernize its legacy platform while introducing a clean‑sheet design. By adapting the GEnx core to two different airframes, Boeing and GE achieve economies of scale in production and support, yet they preserve the specific performance envelope each aircraft demands. For airlines, the overlap can translate into lower inventory costs and streamlined training, though the need for variant‑specific components limits the full extent of simplification. The reported longevity and fuel‑efficiency gains reinforce the GEnx’s role in maintaining the competitiveness of both wide‑bodies in a market increasingly focused on operating cost and environmental impact.
