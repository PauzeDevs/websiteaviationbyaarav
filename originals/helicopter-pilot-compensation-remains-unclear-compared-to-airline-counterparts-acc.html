Helicopter Pilot Compensation Remains Unclear Compared to Airline Counterparts, According to BLS Data

Simple Flying notes that the Bureau of Labor Statistics aggregates helicopter pilots with other commercial pilots, making direct salary comparisons with airline pilots speculative.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-01 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

A Simple Flying analysis highlights that the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) provides median wage figures for airline pilots and a broader commercial‑pilot category that includes helicopter operations, but it does not publish a separate statistic for rotorcraft pilots. The airline median stands at $232,140 (May 2025), while the commercial‑pilot median is $123,220, a figure that blends diverse roles such as charter, corporate, firefighting and helicopter work. The lack of a distinct helicopter‑pilot wage category means that any head‑to‑head salary comparison is based on incomplete data, and the true earnings landscape for helicopter pilots remains opaque.

BLS Salary Figures for Pilots

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median annual salary of $232,140 for occupations classified under airline pilots, copilots and flight engineers as of May 2025. This figure reflects a wide earnings spread: the 10th percentile earns about $106,710, while the 90th percentile reaches $463,830. Within the subset of scheduled air transportation, the median rises to $293,240. In contrast, the BLS lists a median of $123,220 for the commercial‑pilot category, which encompasses 47,630 workers and includes both fixed‑wing and rotary‑wing duties. The 10th percentile in this group earns $58,850 and the 90th percentile $266,620, indicating that even top‑earning commercial pilots typically make less than mid‑range airline pilots.

Why Helicopter Pay Is Not Isolated

Simple Flying points out that the BLS does not maintain a separate occupational code for helicopter pilots. Instead, they fall under the broader commercial‑pilot classification (SOC 53‑2012), which aggregates a variety of non‑airline flying jobs—charter services, corporate aviation, aerial surveying, firefighting, agricultural work, air tours and flight instruction—alongside rotorcraft operations. Because the median wage for this category is a composite of many disparate roles, it cannot accurately represent the earnings of any single segment, including offshore or tour‑operator helicopter pilots. The article stresses that a reliable helicopter‑pilot salary figure would require employer‑specific data rather than reliance on the federal statistics currently available.

Implications for Aspiring Pilots and Industry Stakeholders

The conflation of helicopter and other commercial pilot work creates uncertainty for individuals evaluating career paths. Prospective helicopter pilots cannot rely on the $123,220 median as a definitive benchmark, as the figure blends high‑earning corporate jet captains with lower‑paid agricultural pilots. For employers and policymakers, the absence of a distinct rotorcraft wage statistic hampers efforts to assess labor market health, negotiate compensation packages, or design targeted training incentives. Simple Flying suggests that more granular reporting—either through a dedicated BLS occupational code or through industry surveys—would clarify compensation trends and aid workforce planning.

Key facts

  • The BLS median salary for airline pilots (including copilots and flight engineers) is $232,140 as of May 2025.
  • The commercial‑pilot median, which includes helicopter pilots, is $123,220, covering a heterogeneous mix of jobs.
  • No separate BLS wage category exists for helicopter pilots, making direct salary comparisons speculative.

What this means

The available data illustrate a clear disparity in transparency between airline and helicopter pilot compensation. While airline pilot earnings are tracked with a dedicated occupational code, rotorcraft pilots are subsumed under a broad commercial‑pilot category that masks the variability of their pay. This reporting gap limits the ability of analysts, educators and prospective pilots to gauge market rates for helicopter operations, potentially influencing career decisions and compensation negotiations.
