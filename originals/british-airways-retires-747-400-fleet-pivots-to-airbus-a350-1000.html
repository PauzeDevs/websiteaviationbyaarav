British Airways retires 747‑400 fleet, pivots to Airbus A350‑1000

The UK flag carrier has phased out its historic quad‑jet fleet, replacing it with a modest fleet of twin‑engine A350‑1000s, a shift accelerated by the COVID‑19 pandemic.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-25 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

British Airways, once the world’s largest operator of the Boeing 747‑400, retired its entire 747‑400 fleet during the COVID‑19 pandemic and is now operating 18 Airbus A350‑1000 aircraft. The transition reflects a strategic move toward more fuel‑efficient, twin‑engine aircraft, though the analysis is based solely on reporting by Simple Flying and has not been independently verified.

Background and the end of the quad‑jet era

According to Simple Flying, British Airways built the reputation of the 747‑400 as its flagship wide‑body, at one point operating 57 examples – the largest fleet of that variant worldwide. The airline began a gradual phase‑out of the type before the global health crisis, but the onset of COVID‑19 dramatically accelerated the retirements, resulting in the complete removal of the 747‑400 from the fleet during the pandemic period.

The 747‑400 sub‑fleets and cabin configurations

Simple Flying notes that, in its later years, BA maintained two principal cabin layouts for the 747‑400. The higher‑density version seated 337 passengers (14 First, 52 Club World, 36 World Traveller Plus, 235 World Traveller) and was typically deployed on routes with broader market demand. The premium‑heavy version accommodated 275 passengers (14 First, 86 Club World, 30 World Traveller Plus, 145 World Traveller) and was favoured on premium‑focused services such as the London‑New York corridor, while the denser layout served routes like London‑Las Vegas. The article implies that the premium‑heavy sub‑fleet saw fewer retirements in the 2010s, with most of the remaining 747‑400s withdrawn during the pandemic.

Transition to the Airbus A350‑1000

Simple Flying reports that the Airbus A350‑1000 has become the primary replacement for the retired 747‑400s. The A350‑1000 is described as a large, twin‑engine, carbon‑composite wide‑body aircraft. British Airways currently operates 18 examples of the type. While the A350‑1000 matches the 747‑400 in size, its operational strengths differ, emphasizing fuel efficiency and modern materials over the sheer capacity and four‑engine redundancy that characterised the older quad‑jet.

Strategic implications of the fleet shift

The source suggests that the move to the A350‑1000 aligns with broader industry trends favoring twin‑engine aircraft for long‑haul routes, driven by lower operating costs and regulatory allowances for ETOPS (Extended-range Twin‑engine Operational Performance Standards). The pandemic‑induced fleet reduction provided BA an opportunity to accelerate this transition, shedding older, less efficient aircraft while consolidating capacity in a newer, more versatile platform. However, the article does not provide detailed performance metrics or financial data to quantify the benefits.

Key facts

  • British Airways once operated 57 Boeing 747‑400s, the largest worldwide fleet of that model
  • All 747‑400s were retired during the COVID‑19 pandemic, a process expedited by the health crisis
  • The airline now flies 18 Airbus A350‑1000 aircraft as the primary long‑haul replacement
  • Two main 747‑400 cabin layouts existed: a 337‑seat high‑density version and a 275‑seat premium‑heavy version
  • The A350‑1000 is a twin‑engine, carbon‑composite wide‑body offering different operational strengths than the 747‑400

What this means

Based on the Simple Flying report, British Airways’ decision to replace its quad‑jet fleet with the A350‑1000 appears driven by a combination of pandemic‑related fleet rationalisation and a strategic shift toward more fuel‑efficient, twin‑engine aircraft. The A350‑1000’s modern materials and engine configuration promise lower per‑seat operating costs, which is consistent with industry-wide moves away from four‑engine types. The retention of a modest A350‑1000 fleet suggests BA is balancing capacity needs with the desire for operational flexibility, especially on premium‑heavy routes previously served by the larger 747‑400 configurations.
