Airlines Pivot to 1-2-1 Business Class Configurations on Boeing 787-9 Fleets

Legacy carriers are retrofitting widebody cabins to eliminate the 'step-over' factor and meet evolving corporate travel standards.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-05 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Global operators of the Boeing 787-9 are increasingly abandoning traditional 2-2-2 and 2-3-2 business class layouts in favor of 1-2-1 configurations to provide direct aisle access and enhanced privacy for premium passengers.

The Shift Toward Direct Aisle Access

The aviation industry is witnessing a significant transition in premium cabin design for the Boeing 787-9 Dreamliner. According to Simple Flying, many legacy carriers that initially installed dense 2-2-2 or 2-3-2 seating arrangements are now investing heavily to replace these with 1-2-1 layouts. This shift is primarily motivated by a desire to eliminate the 'step-over' factor, where passengers in window or middle seats must physically navigate over a sleeping neighbor to access the aisle. As premium travel expectations rise, airlines are finding that traditional configurations represent a commercial liability.

Case Study: British Airways Fleet Retrofit

British Airways serves as a prominent example of this industry-wide trend. As reported by Simple Flying, citing information from One Mile at a Time, the airline originally debuted its 787-9 fleet with a 2-3-2 Club World layout that accommodated 42 passengers. To align with modern expectations, the carrier initiated a fleet-wide retrofit program. As noted by Travel Spill, this project replaces the older seats with 38 Club Suites arranged in a 1-2-1 configuration, complete with sliding privacy doors. While this transition resulted in a reduction of four business class seats per aircraft, the airline prioritized the product quality required to remain competitive.

Commercial and Corporate Drivers

Beyond passenger comfort, the move toward 1-2-1 seating is heavily influenced by corporate travel procurement. Simple Flying reports that corporate travel managers are increasingly enforcing strict hardware standards, making direct aisle access a baseline requirement rather than a luxury. Airlines that fail to modernize their widebody cabins risk losing high-yielding business travelers to competitors who offer more private, accessible seating. Consequently, the retrofit process has become a strategic necessity for carriers aiming to retain their share of the premium long-haul market.

Key facts

  • Boeing 787-9 operators are replacing 2-2-2 and 2-3-2 business class layouts with 1-2-1 configurations.
  • British Airways reduced its 787-9 business class capacity from 42 to 38 seats to accommodate the new Club Suites.
  • Direct aisle access is now considered a baseline requirement for premium long-haul travel by many corporate travel managers.
  • The primary driver for the change is the elimination of the 'step-over' factor for passengers in window and middle seats.

What this means

The transition to 1-2-1 seating represents a fundamental shift in airline strategy, where the priority has moved from maximizing seat density to maximizing individual passenger utility. By sacrificing total seat count, airlines are effectively trading volume for higher yields and better alignment with corporate travel policies, suggesting that the 'step-over' design is no longer viable in the modern premium market.
