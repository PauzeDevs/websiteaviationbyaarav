Qantas to train 360+ pilots and 1,200 cabin crew for twelve Airbus A350‑1000ULR aircraft

The Australian carrier announced a massive training effort to support its Project Sunrise ultra‑long‑range fleet, targeting 30 pilots and 100 cabin crew per aircraft.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-07 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Qantas has disclosed plans to prepare more than 360 pilots and 1,200 cabin crew members for operation of a twelve‑aircraft fleet of Airbus A350‑1000ULR jets. The training programme, detailed in a Simple Flying report, reflects the unique demands of nonstop flights exceeding 16,000 km, and is scheduled to culminate with the arrival of the first aircraft in April 2027 and the launch of Sydney‑London services later that year.

Program scale and staffing ratios

According to Simple Flying, Qantas’ own announcement sets the training target at over 360 pilots and 1,200 cabin crew for the twelve A350‑1000ULRs. This translates to an average of 30 pilots and 100 cabin crew per aircraft – roughly double the crew complement typical for conventional long‑haul jets. The airline attributes the inflated ratios to the ultra‑long‑range profile of the aircraft, which includes a rear centre fuel tank that enables flights of up to 22 hours nonstop.

Training rollout and conversion pathway

Training is already underway at Australia’s first A350 simulator located in Sydney, as well as at British Airways’ facilities in the United Kingdom. Initial cohorts are being drawn from Qantas’ existing A330 pilot pool, with about 40 pilots converting to the A350 type while continuing to operate their current aircraft. The programme is designed to align crew readiness with the staggered delivery of the twelve aircraft.

Delivery schedule and route launch

Simple Flying reports that the first A350‑1000ULR is slated to arrive in April 2027. Qantas plans to commence Sydney‑London operations in October 2027, followed by a Sydney‑New York service from mid‑2028. The 238‑seat configuration of each aircraft will support the airline’s Project Sunrise ambition to embed ultra‑long‑range flights as a regular part of its network rather than a one‑off record attempt.

Operational implications

The expanded crew pool of more than 1,560 personnel across the twelve‑aircraft fleet reflects the operational realities of ultra‑long‑range sectors, including longer duty periods, extended rest requirements, and the need for additional pilots to rotate during a single flight. Qantas’ strategy suggests a long‑term commitment to these routes, with staffing levels calibrated to sustain reliability and safety over extended non‑stop legs.

Key facts

  • Qantas aims to train over 360 pilots and 1,200 cabin crew for twelve Airbus A350‑1000ULR jets
  • Each aircraft will carry 238 passengers and feature a rear centre fuel tank for up to 22‑hour nonstop flights
  • Training is being conducted in Sydney’s new A350 simulator and at British Airways’ UK facilities
  • First A350‑1000ULR delivery expected April 2027; Sydney‑London service planned for October 2027

What this means

The reported training scale underscores the logistical challenges of integrating ultra‑long‑range aircraft into a commercial schedule. By allocating twice the typical crew complement per aircraft, Qantas is pre‑emptively addressing fatigue management and regulatory duty‑time constraints inherent to 20‑plus‑hour flights. The dual‑location training approach leverages existing simulator capacity while accelerating conversion pathways for current A330 pilots, suggesting a pragmatic blend of internal resources and external partnerships. If the timeline holds, the rollout could position Qantas as the first major carrier to operate a dedicated fleet of ultra‑long‑range jets on a permanent basis, potentially reshaping competitive dynamics on the Australia‑Europe and Australia‑North America corridors.
