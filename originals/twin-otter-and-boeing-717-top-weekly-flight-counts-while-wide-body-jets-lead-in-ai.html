Twin Otter and Boeing 717 Top Weekly Flight Counts, While Wide‑Body Jets Lead in Airborne Hours

Simple Flying’s flight‑tracker data for a seven‑day period shows a De Havilland Twin Otter and a Hawaiian Airlines 717 each logged 75 flights, whereas an Air New Zealand 787‑9 and an SAS A350‑900 accumulated roughly 134 airborne hours, highlighting the contrast between short‑haul “island hopper” schedules and long‑haul international operations.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-18 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

A Simple Flying analysis of flight‑tracker data for a single week reveals two distinct leaderboards. By number of take‑offs and landings, a Curaçao‑based Divi Divi Air Twin Otter and a Hawaiian Airlines Boeing 717 each completed 75 flights, averaging nearly 11 movements per day on short regional routes. In contrast, the aircraft with the most time in the air were an Air New Zealand Boeing 787‑9 and an SAS Airbus A350‑900, each logging about 134 hours, equivalent to roughly 19 hours daily, on long‑haul routes. The findings underscore how aircraft utilization metrics vary dramatically depending on whether the focus is on flight frequency or total airborne time.

Methodology and Scope

Simple Flying employed its proprietary Flight Tracker to monitor a sample of ten aircraft over a continuous seven‑day window. The analysis split the data into two categories: total number of flights performed and cumulative airborne hours. The sample included a mix of regional turboprops, narrow‑body jets, and wide‑body aircraft, but the selection was not described as exhaustive or statistically representative of global fleets.

Aircraft with the Highest Flight Count

According to Simple Flying, the record for most flights in the week belongs to a De Havilland Canada DHC‑6 Twin Otter operated by Divi Divi Air (registration PJ‑DVF). The aircraft flew 75 legs, primarily shuttling between Curaçao (CUR) and Bonaire (BON) and between Curaçao and Aruba (AUA). A Hawaiian Airlines Boeing 717‑200 (registration N484HA) matched this total, also completing 75 flights across five Hawaiian airports, with the Honolulu‑Lihue corridor accounting for 30 of those movements. Both aircraft averaged close to 11 flights per day, reflecting intensive short‑haul schedules.

Aircraft Dominating Airborne Hours

When measuring total time aloft, Simple Flying identified an Air New Zealand Boeing 787‑9 and an SAS Airbus A350‑900 as the leaders, each accruing roughly 134 hours in the seven‑day span. This translates to about 19 hours of flight per day, indicating that these wide‑body jets were engaged in long‑distance international services rather than frequent short hops. The report notes that all ten aircraft in the airborne‑hours ranking were wide‑bodies, contrasting with the flight‑count list, which comprised only regional aircraft.

Interpretation of the Dual Rankings

The divergent leaderboards illustrate how utilization can be framed. High flight counts favor aircraft operating short sectors with rapid turn‑arounds, typical of island‑hopping services. Conversely, total airborne time highlights the efficiency of long‑haul aircraft that spend extended periods in the sky, even if they execute fewer individual flights. For airlines, the choice of metric influences fleet planning, crew scheduling, and revenue strategies. The Simple Flying data suggests that both operational models can achieve high utilization, albeit measured differently.

Key facts

  • Divi Divi Air’s Twin Otter and Hawaiian Airlines’ Boeing 717 each logged 75 flights in a single week, the highest counts observed.
  • Both aircraft averaged nearly 11 flights per day, operating on short regional routes in the Caribbean and Hawaii.
  • An Air New Zealand Boeing 787‑9 and an SAS Airbus A350‑900 each recorded about 134 airborne hours, roughly 19 hours per day.
  • The flight‑count leaders were turboprop and narrow‑body jets, while the airborne‑hour leaders were wide‑body long‑haul aircraft.

What this means

The Simple Flying findings demonstrate that utilization metrics are highly context‑dependent. Short‑haul operators can achieve impressive flight frequencies, which may benefit slot‑constrained airports and generate steady ancillary revenue. Long‑haul carriers, meanwhile, maximize aircraft value through prolonged flight time, supporting higher passenger‑kilometre yields. Stakeholders should therefore align performance benchmarks with their specific network strategy rather than relying on a single utilization figure.

What we could not verify

The analysis is based solely on Simple Flying’s proprietary flight‑tracker data for a single week and a limited sample of ten aircraft. No independent verification of the flight counts or airborne‑hour totals is provided, and the selection criteria for the aircraft are not disclosed. Consequently, the results may not be representative of broader industry patterns, and the absence of corroborating sources introduces uncertainty about the exact figures.
