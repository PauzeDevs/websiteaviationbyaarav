US airlines plan larger first‑class cabins on the newly certified Boeing 737 MAX 10

Four major U.S. carriers intend to equip the incoming 737 MAX 10 with 24 first‑class seats, a step up from the 20‑seat layouts previously expected, as they chase growing premium demand.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-15 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that the Boeing 737 MAX 10 has received certification and will soon enter service with several North American airlines. American, Delta, Alaska and United have announced cabin configurations that increase first‑class capacity to 24 seats, with Alaska also targeting lie‑flat business seats. The moves reflect a broader strategy to capture higher‑yield passengers on short‑ and medium‑haul routes.

Certification and delivery schedule

According to Simple Flying, the 737 MAX 10 has achieved type certification, clearing the way for deliveries to begin in 2027. WestJet is slated to be the launch operator, while Alaska Airlines and United Airlines are expected to receive their first aircraft shortly thereafter. Delta plans to start taking deliveries later in 2027, and American’s first MAX 10s are projected for 2029. The four U.S. airlines together have ordered 550 units, underscoring the model’s importance to their narrow‑body fleets.

Revised first‑class configurations

Both American Airlines and Delta Air Lines have publicly confirmed that their MAX 10s will feature 24 first‑class seats, up from the earlier expectation of 20. Delta previously described a layout of 20 Delta First seats and 182 total seats, but recent statements from company leadership revised the first‑class count to 24. American’s announcement similarly cites a 24‑seat first‑class cabin together with an expanded Main Cabin Extra section. Alaska Airlines is pursuing a different approach, planning a sub‑fleet of MAX 10s equipped with lie‑flat business‑class seats aimed at competing directly with legacy carriers on transcontinental routes.

Competitive and fleet context

Within the U.S. market, the largest existing first‑class cabins on narrow‑body aircraft belong to the Airbus A321‑200 and A321neo, each offering 20 seats. Legacy carriers Delta and United operate the larger Boeing 757‑300 with 24 first‑class seats, but that aircraft is significantly bigger than the A321 or the MAX 10. By installing 24 first‑class seats on a narrow‑body platform, the airlines aim to provide a premium product comparable to the 757‑300 while retaining the operational efficiencies of a smaller aircraft.

Market drivers behind the expansion

Simple Flying attributes the decision to enlarge first‑class cabins to rising demand for premium travel. The outlet notes that first‑class seats are experiencing high load factors, prompting airlines to increase capacity in that segment to capture additional revenue. The MAX 10’s size, comparable to the A321neo, allows carriers to add more premium seats without sacrificing overall capacity, aligning with their broader strategy to maximize yield on short‑ and medium‑haul routes.

Key facts

  • Boeing 737 MAX 10 has been certified and will enter service with U.S. airlines starting in 2027
  • American and Delta will each install 24 first‑class seats on their MAX 10s, up from the previously expected 20
  • Alaska plans a MAX 10 sub‑fleet with lie‑flat business‑class seats for transcontinental competition
  • Four U.S. carriers have collectively ordered 550 MAX 10 aircraft

What this means

The shift to larger first‑class cabins on the MAX 10 suggests that U.S. carriers view the narrow‑body market as a viable arena for premium revenue, traditionally dominated by larger aircraft. By leveraging the MAX 10’s capacity, airlines can offer a high‑margin product while maintaining lower operating costs than wide‑body or older wide‑body jets. Alaska’s focus on lie‑flat seats indicates a targeted effort to challenge legacy carriers on long‑haul domestic routes, where business travelers value comfort. Overall, the strategy reflects a broader industry trend of extracting more premium yield from short‑haul networks.
