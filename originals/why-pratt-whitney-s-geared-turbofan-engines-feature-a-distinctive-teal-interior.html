Why Pratt & Whitney’s Geared Turbofan Engines Feature a Distinctive Teal Interior

An examination of Simple Flying’s explanation reveals that the green‑tinged ring inside GTF nacelles serves acoustic, thermal and inspection purposes, not mere branding.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-21 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that the teal band seen inside the fan case of Pratt & Whitney’s PW1100G and PW1500G engines is a multi‑function acoustic liner, a heat‑resistant coating for tip‑clearance monitoring, and a visual aid for maintenance crews. The article links the coloration to the engine’s geared architecture, which shifts noise frequencies and demands larger resonant chambers. The same source notes that the hue persists because it simplifies visual inspections, though no other outlet has independently confirmed these details.

A noticeable teal ring on the fan case

Spotters and passengers on Airbus A320neo and A220 aircraft have long reported a consistent teal‑green line inside the engine nacelle, just ahead of the fan blades. Simple Flying identifies this feature on both the PW1100G and PW1500G variants, indicating that the coloration is a fleet‑wide design element rather than an isolated paint job.

Acoustic liner engineered for low‑frequency noise

According to Simple Flying, the primary component of the green band is an acoustic liner composed of a perforated skin bonded to a honeycomb core. The perforations allow fan‑generated sound waves to enter the honeycomb cells, where they are converted to heat. Because the geared turbofan’s gearbox permits the fan to rotate more slowly, the engine emits a greater proportion of low‑frequency noise, which requires deeper resonant cavities for effective attenuation. The liner’s size and cell geometry are therefore tuned to target those lower frequencies, and the teal pigment results from the material used in the liner’s construction.

Thermal coating and tip‑clearance monitoring

Simple Flying adds that a second green‑colored element is a heat‑resistant coating applied to the inner fan case. This coating protects the metal from the high temperatures generated by the gearbox and turbine sections, while also providing a visual reference for technicians checking tip‑clearance gaps during routine inspections. The contrast between the coating and the surrounding metal makes it easier to spot wear or deformation.

Practical benefits for maintenance crews

The article notes a third reason for the persistence of the teal hue: it aids visual inspections. Technicians can quickly verify the integrity of the acoustic liner and thermal coating without removing the fan case, reducing inspection time and the risk of inadvertent damage. Simple Flying suggests that this practical advantage has reinforced the continued use of the green material across new production engines.

Key facts

  • The teal ring is an acoustic liner designed to absorb low‑frequency fan noise specific to geared turbofans.
  • A heat‑resistant coating behind the liner also appears green and assists in tip‑clearance checks.
  • The coloration simplifies visual inspections, helping maintenance crews identify wear without disassembly.

What this means

If Simple Flying’s description is accurate, the green interior reflects a purposeful integration of acoustic, thermal and operational considerations rather than a cosmetic choice. By embedding a tuned acoustic liner and a durable coating that are both visually distinct, Pratt & Whitney appears to have streamlined both performance optimization and maintenance efficiency. This multi‑layered approach could influence future engine designs, especially as manufacturers seek to balance noise reduction mandates with cost‑effective servicing.

What we could not verify

The report relies exclusively on a single Simple Flying article; no independent technical documentation, manufacturer statements, or third‑party analyses were provided to corroborate the claims. Consequently, the explanations of the acoustic liner’s tuning, the thermal coating’s composition, and the inspection benefits remain unverified beyond the source’s account.
