Study Finds 97% of Boeing 777 Captains Persist with Unstable Approaches

Simple Flying reports that an overwhelming majority of Boeing 777 crews land despite not meeting established stable‑approach criteria, highlighting a gap between SOPs and pilot behaviour.

A recent Simple Flying article cites data indicating that 97 % of Boeing 777 pilots continue an unstable approach rather than executing a go‑around. The piece references the Flight Safety Foundation’s 1998 ALAR toolkit, which defines stable‑approach parameters such as altitude, flight path, speed and configuration. Stable‑Approach Standards and Their Origin

The concept of a "stable approach" was formalised by the Flight Safety Foundation (FSF) in its 1998 Approach and Landing Accident Reduction (ALAR) toolkit. The FSF recommends that, before reaching a prescribed minimum altitude—1,000 feet (305 m) in instrument conditions or 500 feet (152 m) in visual conditions—aircraft must satisfy a checklist. The checklist includes maintaining the correct flight path, keeping airspeed within a band of the reference landing speed (VREF) plus or minus 20 knots, being in the proper landing configuration, and adhering to an acceptable rate of descent. Small tolerances are allowed for speed and sink rate, but the overall intent is to ensure only minor control adjustments are needed at the final phase of the flight.

Reported Continuation Rate Among Boeing 777 Crews

According to Simple Flying, an analysis of Boeing 777 operations shows that 97 % of pilots who encounter an unstable approach elect to continue the landing rather than initiate a go‑around. The article stresses that, while most landings are completed under stable conditions, the disparity between prescribed SOPs and actual pilot choices is stark. The source does not provide raw data tables, sample sizes, or the methodology used to derive the 97 % figure, and it acknowledges that the observation is based on internal airline reporting rather than an external audit.

Human‑Factor Influences on Decision‑Making

The Simple Flying piece attributes the high continuation rate to psychological pressures that arise in the brief window between recognizing an unstable approach and reaching the minimum altitude. Factors cited include perceived workload, fear of disrupting schedules, and a cultural bias toward completing the landing. The article suggests that these human‑factor elements can outweigh the clear procedural guidance that calls for an immediate go‑around when criteria are not met.

Safety Implications and Industry Response

If the reported continuation rate is accurate, it signals a systemic gap between airline SOPs and pilot execution. The article implies that airlines may need to reinforce training on go‑around decision points, incorporate more realistic simulator scenarios, and address organisational culture that discourages aborting a landing. No specific regulatory response or industry‑wide initiative is mentioned in the source material.

What this means

The data presented by Simple Flying suggests a pronounced disconnect between documented stable‑approach SOPs and actual pilot behaviour on Boeing 777s. Assuming the 97 % figure reflects a broad sample, the trend points to a safety culture where procedural compliance is compromised by short‑term operational pressures. This could elevate the risk of approach‑and‑landing incidents, especially in marginal weather, and underscores the need for targeted interventions—such as reinforced go‑around training and cultural shifts that normalise aborting a landing when criteria are unmet.
