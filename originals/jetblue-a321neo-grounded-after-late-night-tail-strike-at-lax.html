JetBlue A321neo grounded after late‑night tail strike at LAX

A two‑year‑old Airbus A321‑neo suffered rear‑fuselage contact during a go‑around on Nov. 22, prompting FAA documentation and flight cancellations, according to Simple Flying.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-24 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that JetBlue Flight B6524, operating an Airbus A321‑neo (tail N2193J), struck its tail while executing a go‑around at Los Angeles International Airport on the night of Nov. 22, 2026. The aircraft was subsequently taken out of service pending damage assessment. The incident adds to a series of recent tail‑strike events involving JetBlue aircraft, though no injuries have been reported in any case.

Incident timeline and immediate response

According to Simple Flying, the JetBlue A321‑neo was on final approach to Runway 25L at LAX when the crew initiated a go‑around at approximately 11:44 p.m. local time. During the climb‑out, the underside of the rear fuselage contacted the runway surface, producing visible sparks that were captured on a live video feed. The pilots recovered the aircraft, climbed away, and re‑approached for a second landing, touching down safely at about 12:01 a.m. without further incident.

Aircraft details and operational fallout

The aircraft involved is a 2024‑delivered Airbus A321‑271NX, registered N2193J and nicknamed "StateMint Piece." Simple Flying notes that the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) entered a preliminary incident report noting the tail‑strike, but the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) has not yet opened a formal investigation. The jet’s scheduled return flight to New York‑JFK (Flight B6524) slated for 2:30 p.m. was cancelled while the plane remains grounded for damage assessment.

Recent JetBlue tail‑strike history

Simple Flying places the LAX event in the context of two prior tail‑strike incidents involving JetBlue aircraft. In February of the same year, an A321‑neo (tail N2188J) struck its tail on Runway 28L at San Francisco International Airport during landing; an NTSB report cited a bounce and aggressive aft‑sidestick input by the first officer as contributing factors. An earlier 2022 incident involved an Airbus A320 (tail N760JB) that hit its tail during a rapid departure from Hayden, Colorado, after a coordination error with a Beechcraft B300 King Air. No injuries were reported in any of the three cases.

Safety considerations and next steps

While Simple Flying emphasizes that no passengers or crew were harmed, the recurrence of tail‑strike events on JetBlue’s narrow‑body fleet raises questions about procedural adherence during go‑arounds and low‑speed departures. The FAA’s preliminary report suggests the incident will be examined for possible operational or training implications, though the lack of an NTSB investigation at this stage limits public insight into root causes. JetBlue has not released a statement beyond the flight cancellation, and the extent of structural damage remains undisclosed.

What this means

The LAX tail‑strike adds to a pattern of rear‑fuselage contacts on JetBlue’s A321 family, suggesting a possible systemic issue with go‑around execution or low‑speed handling. While the immediate outcome was benign—no injuries and a safe subsequent landing—the repeated nature of such events may prompt regulators and the airline to review pilot training, especially on pitch‑control inputs during aborted landings. The absence of an NTSB investigation at this point limits the depth of technical analysis, but the FAA’s preliminary documentation indicates that the incident will be scrutinized for procedural compliance.
