Air Canada 737 MAX 8 receives TCAS climb advisory after unresponsive Cessna near Palm Springs

A routine climb out of Palm Springs International Airport triggered a traffic‑collision‑avoidance warning when a small Cessna failed to respond, prompting the 737 MAX 8 to climb an extra 600 feet before continuing to Vancouver.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-23 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

On 17 November 2026, Air Canada flight AC1769, a Boeing 737 MAX 8 bound for Vancouver, was issued a Traffic Collision Avoidance System (TCAS) resolution advisory while climbing through roughly 11,400 feet after an unidentified Cessna did not answer radio calls. The advisory instructed the crew to climb to 12,000 feet, a maneuver they executed without diverting. The aircraft completed its scheduled flight and landed safely in Vancouver. Details such as the Cessna’s registration, exact proximity, and the cause of its non‑response were not disclosed, and the incident remains reported solely by Simple Flying.

Incident overview

According to Simple Flying, the Air Canada 737 MAX 8 departed Palm Springs International Airport at 12:45 p.m. local time on 17 November 2026, heading for Vancouver International Airport. While climbing out of runway 31R, the aircraft’s onboard TCAS detected a potential conflict with a small Cessna that was not responding to communications. The system generated a resolution advisory, directing the pilots to increase altitude to 12,000 feet to achieve vertical separation.

Flight details and crew response

The flight, registered C‑FSDB, complied with the TCAS instruction, climbing an additional 600 feet before resuming its climb to cruising altitude. The crew reported that the advisory was a climb‑only resolution, not a proximity warning, and no further evasive action was required. After the maneuver, the flight continued on schedule and landed at Vancouver without any diversion or delay, as noted by Simple Flying.

Open questions and safety context

Simple Flying highlighted that several critical pieces of information remain unavailable. The identity of the Cessna, the exact distance between the two aircraft at the time of the advisory, and the reason for the Cessna’s lack of response were not disclosed. The report underscores the role of TCAS in preventing mid‑air collisions, especially when one aircraft is uncommunicative, but also points to the need for more transparent post‑incident data to assess systemic safety implications.

What this means

The incident illustrates how TCAS can provide decisive guidance even when one aircraft is silent, allowing the larger, commercial aircraft to maintain safety without interrupting its schedule. However, the lack of publicly available data on the smaller aircraft limits a full safety assessment. Transparency about the proximity and cause of non‑response would enable regulators and operators to evaluate whether procedural changes or additional monitoring are warranted.
