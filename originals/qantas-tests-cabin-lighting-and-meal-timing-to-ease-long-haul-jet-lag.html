Qantas Tests Cabin Lighting and Meal Timing to Ease Long‑Haul Jet Lag

A decade‑long research programme by Qantas, under Project Sunrise, explores how adjusted lighting, sleep schedules and meal composition may lessen the physiological strain of ultra‑long flights.

AVIATION BY AARAV NEWSROOM · 2026-11-11 · 1 SOURCE · SINGLE-SOURCE ANALYSIS

At a glance

Simple Flying reports that Qantas has spent more than ten years investigating whether strategic changes to cabin lighting, meal timing and food composition can mitigate jet lag on ultra‑long‑haul routes. The airline’s Project Sunrise research flights in 2019, involving 23 volunteers equipped with wearable sensors, tested alternative inflight routines against conventional patterns. Preliminary findings released in 2023 indicated that passengers experienced milder jet‑lag symptoms when exposed to tailored lighting, altered sleep windows, specific menu items and scheduled movement. The evidence remains early and has not been independently verified.

Why Jet Lag Matters to Airlines

Jet lag arises when a traveller’s internal circadian rhythm is out of sync with the destination’s day‑night cycle, leading to fatigue, reduced cognitive performance and discomfort. For airlines operating ultra‑long routes, passenger well‑being directly influences brand perception and operational efficiency. The Simple Flying article notes that, with newer aircraft and advanced cabin systems, airlines are now able to experiment with environmental controls that could potentially alleviate these effects.

Project Sunrise: A Decade of Research

Qantas launched Project Sunrise as a platform to develop the world’s first nonstop commercial services from Sydney to London and New York. According to Simple Flying, the programme began in 2015 in partnership with the University of Sydney’s Charles Perkins Centre. In 2019 the airline conducted three research flights from New York and London to Sydney, each carrying 23 volunteer passengers. Participants wore wearable devices that recorded physiological data throughout the roughly 20‑hour journeys. The flights incorporated a deliberately altered schedule of lighting, sleep, meals and movement, contrasting with the traditional inflight routine of eating and sleeping at airline‑determined times.

Preliminary Findings Suggest Benefit

The research, whose results were released in 2023, indicated several positive trends. Simple Flying reports that passengers on the experimental flights reported less severe jet‑lag symptoms. Specific adjustments—such as exposure to lighting that mimics natural daylight, timed sleep periods, inclusion of ingredients like chili and chocolate, and scheduled out‑of‑seat activity—were associated with improved subjective well‑being. The study also highlighted the importance of movement and exercise as factors that may help the body clock adjust more quickly to a new time zone.

Implications and Remaining Questions

If the early evidence holds, airlines could integrate circadian‑aligned lighting systems, flexible meal service and structured activity periods into regular long‑haul operations. Such changes could enhance passenger comfort, reduce fatigue‑related incidents, and differentiate carriers in a competitive market. However, the Simple Flying piece emphasizes that the data set is limited to a small volunteer group and that the findings are still considered "early". Broader validation across diverse passenger demographics, different aircraft types and varied route structures remains necessary before industry‑wide adoption.

What this means

The Simple Flying report provides a rare glimpse into how an airline is applying human‑factors research to the inflight environment. By aligning cabin lighting and service schedules with circadian principles, Qantas aims to reshape the passenger experience on ultra‑long routes. While the reported outcomes are promising, the limited sample size and lack of independent corroboration mean that conclusions must remain tentative. Should larger‑scale trials confirm these early signals, the approach could become a new standard for airlines seeking to differentiate their long‑haul product and improve safety outcomes linked to passenger fatigue.
